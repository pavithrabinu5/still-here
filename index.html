Still Here

    Sea of Tranquility · 20 July 1969

    The box in his right hand
    is still working.

    Buzz Aldrin is carrying two experiments. The one in his left hand needed solar power and fell silent in 1969. The one in his right hand has no battery, no solar panel and no radio — and scientists on Earth still use it .

    How can equipment left on the Moon decades ago still help us do science — without any electrical power there?

    Laser Ranging Retro-Reflector

    • Power None — it “required no power”
    • Radio None. It sends no data of its own.
    • Made of 100 glass “corner cubes”
    • Placed About 5 m north-north-west of the seismometer

    Both were part of a small package called EASEP, the forerunner of later Apollo science stations .

      Chapter 2 · The trick in the glass

      A mirror that sends light back the way it came.

      Nobody on the Moon aims the reflector. The Moon moves, Earth turns, and the laser station is in a different place every night. So how does the light find its way home? Change the angle of the incoming beam.

      Illustration · 2-D slice. A real corner cube uses three mirror faces at right angles.

      Reflector type

      In the corner, light bounces off one face, then the other. Two right-angled bounces reverse its direction exactly, whatever the angle. Each Apollo 11 corner cube does this with three faces, in 3-D . That is the whole “machine”: shaped glass, with nothing to switch on.

      Chapter 3 · The round trip

      All the timing happens on Earth.

      A laser station such as Apache Point in New Mexico fires a short pulse through a 3.5-metre telescope . The station notes when the pulse leaves and when light comes back. The reflector records nothing.

      • Right · Reflector on the Moon glass only, no power

      Station stopwatch (illustrative) 0.000 s

      The animation runs for the round-trip time you choose below, in real time. The picture is squeezed: the real Moon is much farther away, compared with the size of Earth, than it looks here.

      NASA describes the round trip as taking “about 2.5 seconds on average” . It varies because the Moon’s distance changes. These values are for practice — they are not real observations.

      distance = speed of light × time ÷ 2

      — km, Earth station to reflector

      Why divide by two?

      The stopwatch runs while the light goes out and back. That is twice the distance. Half the time is the trip to the Moon.

      What this simple formula leaves out. Real lunar laser ranging must account for light slowed and scattered by Earth’s atmosphere (twice), and for the exact, constantly changing positions of the station and the reflector . Stations combine many returns and calibrate their equipment . That is how they reach millimetre-level precision . This page cannot do that.

      Chapter 4 · From echo to evidence

      Catching light is only the first step.

      Light reaching the Moon spreads out — from a few metres wide on the ground to more than 2 km at the Moon . By some estimates, only about 1 in 25 million photons hits the Apollo 11 reflector. Only about 1 in 250 million of those makes it back . So how do a few returning photons become knowledge? Step through the work.

      Detect a returning photon

      Apache Point uses a large telescope and sensitive light detectors called avalanche photodiodes to catch the returning photons .

      One return can tell you: light made the trip, and roughly how long it took. It cannot tell you that the Moon is drifting away, or what is inside it.

      Calibrate the instrument

      The station’s own equipment adds tiny timing delays. In late 2016 Apache Point installed an Absolute Calibration System. Later, the team reprocessed all of the station’s data to make it more accurate .

      A measurement is only as good as your knowledge of your own instrument.

      Check quality and combine returns

      Raw detections are reduced into summary measurements called “normal points”. NASA’s team added a quality-control process to find centimetre-level biases and remove bad points before publishing .

      Some reflectors now return only about a tenth of the expected signal. Dust is one suspected cause, still being studied .

      Repeat — for decades, from many places

      Apache Point ranges several times a month to all five reflector arrays on the Moon: three placed by Apollo astronauts and two on the Soviet Lunokhod rovers . Its data from 2006 to early 2024 are now in a public NASA archive .

      “Laser-ranging science is a long game.” — Erwan Mazarico, NASA Goddard

      Interpret with models

      Over many years and several reflectors, scientists found that the Moon is slowly moving away from Earth, about 3.8 cm per year. They also found signs that the Moon has a fluid core, from tiny wobbles in its rotation .

      These results come from long records across several reflectors and stations — not from Apollo 11 alone, and never from a single pulse.

      Epilogue

      Still here.

      The hardware

      100 glass corner cubes, set down by Buzz Aldrin in 1969. No battery, no radio, nothing to switch on .

      The people

      On Earth, teams keep the lasers, telescopes, detectors and calibration running. At Apache Point, New Mexico State University runs daily operations for the NASA Space Geodesy Network .

      The knowledge

      Decades of careful, repeated measurements to several reflectors have shown the Moon drifting away, and given clues to its insides .

      The answer to our question: the reflector doesn’t need power because it only gives back light. The energy, the timing and the science all happen on Earth. The reflector just has to stay where it was put — and it has.
